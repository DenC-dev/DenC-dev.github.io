Where do Idaté and Lakalei belong? A pre-registered lexical-phylogenetic test of two classifications of Timor’s Austronesian languages

Liabut. Phaistos Labs working paper, version 4, November 2026 (not peer reviewed)

paper / supplement / website / code and data / how to reproduce

Two published classifications disagree about where Idaté and Lakalei, two small languages of Timor-Leste, belong. I compared word lists from across the island to see which one the vocabulary backs, and wrote down my tests and how I’d read them before running anything. The pre-registered verdict is unresolved: the lexical evidence does not reliably distinguish a Central Timor relationship from contact or shared retentions. Split-network analysis places Lakalei–Idaté with Central Timor in 47% of bootstrap networks and associates Idaté with Habu in 45%, so the vocabulary does not justify choosing either classification.

Recovering Timucua: A Computer-Assisted Framework for Text Analysis and Contact Detection in Mikasuki and Muscogee

Phaistos Labs research proposal and pilot database, 2026

website / database / CLDF / SQLite

Timucua was spoken in northern Florida until the 1700s, and Spanish missionaries wrote down a surprising amount of it. I wanted to know whether it left any words behind in Mikasuki or Muscogee, the languages of the Seminole and Miccosukee. Across the pilot’s 32 tests, the available wordlists show no statistically detectable Timucua-derived layer in Mikasuki, Creek, or Apalachee. This is evidence against substantial borrowing detectable in the surviving data, not proof that no individual words ever transferred: the method also misses some documented contact cases.

Pilot data and results

Background

The 1612 catechisms are the earliest writing in any Native language of what is now the United States. There are also two letters written by Timucua speakers in the mid-1600s, and George Aaron Broadwell published a full reference grammar in 2024. If Timucua words did make it into Mikasuki or Muscogee, it most likely happened when Georgia groups took Timucua captives (about 1659 to 1715) or when the Seminole settled Florida (about 1750 to 1763). Comparing Georgia-era Hitchiti with Florida Mikasuki is how I try to tell those apart.

The project’s other half, automatically glossing the Timucua texts, is waiting on access to Broadwell’s corpus. The code to score it is already in the repo.

Findings

  • Mikasuki shows no Timucua layer (z = 0.64, p = 0.26). Timucua comes in 29th of 51 possible donor languages, and Creek and every robustness check say the same thing.
  • That null only goes so far. The same test easily picks up inherited words and a pidgin, but it also misses the documented Yuchi–Creek and Natchez–Creek contact, so it can only rule out a large layer.
  • Three Mikasuki words with no match anywhere else in Muskogean (who, tree, fire) already show up in 1880s Hitchiti, so they were there before the move to Florida.
  • Apalachee shows nothing either (z = −0.70).
  • The one interesting lead goes the other way. Timucua efa ‘dog’, neha ‘grease’ and ilake ‘night’ could have come from Muskogean. I didn’t plan to test that, so for now it’s just a lead.
  • A side result on method: a plain permutation test calls 8 to 30% of unrelated language pairs related. The two-way rank rule I used cuts that to about 2%.

Reading the Khitan small script

Early stage, 2026

starting point: Kane, The Kitan Language and Script (2009)

The Khitan small script was used during the Liao dynasty (916–1125) in northern China, and only part of it can be read today. Its language was probably a distant relative of Mongolian. I’m just getting started, working from Kane’s readings and seeing how much further NLP methods, Mongolic cognates and the Chinese texts written alongside Khitan ones can take them.
